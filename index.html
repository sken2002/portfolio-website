The work, with the parts that went wrong

02 — Experience

Anyone can list outcomes. These include the alternative I rejected and what broke in production, because that is the part that tells you whether I was actually there.

Three engagements, one thread

Every one of them came down to the same thing: somebody had data they could not act on, and needed a person who could go all the way from the storage layer to the slide. That is the job I want to keep doing.

Data & AI Consultant

Thorogood Associates — Bangalore, India
2023 – 2025 · Full-time

Developer and de-facto lead on a live platform for an FCA-regulated ~£6bn fund, and owner of a customer P&L data product for a FTSE-100 multinational.

Context
A live platform for an FCA-regulated ~£6bn fund. I was developer and de-facto lead — L3 owner — which meant production ownership and being the client's trusted final check before anything shipped.
Constraint
A regulated environment. Cash-flow, XIRR, liquidity and valuation logic have to be right, and "right" means auditable rather than approximately correct.
What I built
40+ features across that logic, and 20+ production data pipelines on Azure Databricks. Separately, I automated a manual pricing process directly into the client's enterprise SAP.
Alternative I rejected

Two of the client's internal tools had already been half-modernised: the SSIS packages and the C#/.NET front end were in source control and running through CI/CD. The SQL layer — the tables, stored procedures and views that everything actually sat on — had never been put in a repo at all. It was still being changed directly on the server.

The path of least resistance was to leave it that way and keep adding features on top. Nobody was asking me to change it. I turned that down and pushed to bring the SQL into version control and CI/CD alongside the rest. Short-term pain for long-term gain: slower delivery for a stretch, and a fair amount of persuading. But shipping features onto an unversioned database is how you end up unable to reproduce your own production environment. I got it done before I left.

What broke

The analytics cube stopped refreshing. The cause was a stored procedure computing XIRR across every fund — it had grown slow enough that the refresh no longer fit inside its window, so the whole cube would overrun and fail to process.

I brought that procedure's runtime down, but the bigger fix was re-sequencing the table refreshes so the cube's dependencies completed in an order that shortened the overall window rather than just the one procedure. The slow query is the symptom you can see, not necessarily the constraint that is actually binding.

Result
The pricing automation removed ~500 hours a year of manual work. I self-sourced a ~£70,000 opportunity by spotting reusable data nobody had flagged. The customer P&L product integrated 18+ data sources into daily dashboards a FTSE-100 consumer-healthcare multinational's commercial teams decided on, with me as sole client contact.
Also shipped
A three-day SQL and data-modelling training delivered to 40+ new joiners.
Stack
Python · SQL Server · T-SQL · SSIS · SSAS cubes · PySpark · Azure Databricks · Power BI · C#/.NET · CI/CD · SAP integration

The Sports Consultancy — London
2025 – Feb 2026 · Part-time, alongside the MAM

Sole data lead for a global sports consultancy, part-time, while doing a full-time Masters.

Context
~19 years of siloed, unstructured records spread across systems and formats — the accumulated exhaust of a firm that had been busy rather than tidy.
Constraint
Part-time hours, alongside a full-time Masters, as the only data person in the building.
What I built
A single consolidated, analysis-ready asset out of those nineteen years.
The hard part

The auditing. Nineteen years of files in different formats, different structures and different conventions, with no reliable record of what existed or what any of it meant. There was no clever technique available — it was opening things, cataloguing them, and working out what each one actually was.

It was the least interesting part of the engagement and it took the most time, which is usually the honest answer about data work. What came out of it was a proposal for getting from nothing to a first structured baseline: what to keep, what to reorganise, and in what order.

AI Risk Intelligence

EY — via LBS LondonLAB
2026 · Eight weeks · Team of five

Sole technical builder of an AI risk-intelligence tool over a synthetic £1.13bn portfolio.

Context
Portfolio and capital-project risk shows up in the financials long before anyone escalates it. The question was whether it could be surfaced early enough to act on.
Constraint
Synthetic data only, and an output a partner could read in ninety seconds without being asked to trust a black box.
What I built
A Composite Risk Index and a graph-based model, as sole technical builder. I also led delivery for a team of five on an Agile board across eight weeks, turning an ambiguous brief into scoped, on-time work.
Design tension

The obvious alternative was a tabular risk register — each work package scored independently, rolled up into a portfolio average, red/amber/green. That is how most project dashboards work.

We rejected it because infrastructure risk is systemic rather than isolated. A table can tell you a work package has a poor Cost Performance Index. A graph lets you quantify blast radius: a breadth-first traversal showed that one failing work package cascaded three levels deep, hit six downstream entities, and pushed the go-live milestone out by 54 days. You cannot run that on a flat table — you need the topology.

What we gave up:

  • Lenient ingestion. A tabular dashboard needs entities and scores. The graph needs explicit, correct dependency edges — if the source data does not link a contract to a milestone, the cascade simply is not predicted.
  • Familiarity. Project directors read spreadsheets and top-ten lists, not force-directed networks. We built a five-step investigation workflow so the graph did not just land as spaghetti.
  • Cheap arithmetic. No SUM and GROUP BY. Every mitigation lever ran an O(V+E) traversal client-side to recompute cascading exposure live.
Result
Commended by faculty and presented to the client sponsor.

04 — Runnable

Written for this page as a companion to the geopolitical risk tracker — same technique, stripped to something you can interrogate in a browser. A three-regime Markov decision process with rebalancing costs. Because trading is not free, the best allocation depends on what you already hold, which is what makes this a control problem rather than a sequence of independent optimisations. It solves by value iteration over 198 states, then simulates 30 years against a static 60/40. No server, no network call.

How to read this. The state is (regime, portfolio you currently hold) — 3 × 66 = 198 states, with 66 candidate allocations on a 10% simplex grid. Value iteration runs to convergence, then the policy is applied forward through a seeded 30-year simulation, so the path is identical on every reload and the only thing that moves it is you. The reason holdings belong in the state: with zero rebalancing cost the problem decomposes into 3 independent one-period optimisations and the discount rate becomes irrelevant. Set the cost slider to zero and watch γ go inert; that degeneracy is the point.

What this deliberately does not claim. The chart is one path — the headline statistics are medians over 120 independent draws, because a single seed is an anecdote. The policy also observes the regime exactly, which is the strongest assumption here: in practice you would infer it from noisy data with a hidden Markov filter, and most of the apparent edge would go to paying for that uncertainty. Parameters are synthetic and chosen to be plausible, not fitted to history.
